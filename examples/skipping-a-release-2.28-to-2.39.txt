Worked example output: glibc-2.28 (RHEL8) -> glibc-2.39 (RHEL10), skipping 2.34

NOT AN AUDITED PAIR, and not a published verdict. This project audits
RHEL8 -> RHEL9 and RHEL9 -> RHEL10, and publishes results for those two only.
This file is kept for one reason: it is the pair whose intermediate version
this project has itself audited, so a direct jump can be checked against the
sum of the two steps -- and that is the only way to find out whether a jump
reports less than the steps it replaces.

See ../docs/method.md, "How far apart the two tags may be" -- this file is the
third run of the triple that section is measured on.

It answers a question the documentation used to leave open. The README called
the audited pairs "the two adjacent upgrades", which reads as a rule about
what you may pass rather than a statement about what has been measured. There
is no adjacency check anywhere in the tool: the only thing refused is a
reversed pair.

Note what "adjacent" was ever describing. The audited pairs are consecutive
RHEL majors, not consecutive glibc releases: 2.28 -> 2.34 leaves out five
upstream releases and 2.34 -> 2.39 leaves out four. This pair leaves out ten.
It is a wider case of what every published result already is.

    --old-locales-dir ./el8-locales  --old-build-id glibc-2.28-251.el8_10.40 \

made worse by skipping a release.

THE ONE FIGURE THAT IS NOT A SUM: 75, NOT 76

The two-step reading counts one hunk twice. It is in
locale/programs/localedef.c, and it is the copyright string:

  2.28 -> 2.34    -"), "2018");        2.34 -> 2.39    -"), "2021");
                  +"), "2021");                        +"), "2024");

Read end to end that is one change, "2018" -> "2024", and the direct run counts
it once. The direct figure is the right one; the sum over-counts.

WHY THIS IS STRUCTURAL AND NOT LUCK

Steps 1, 2 and 5 are a git diff between the two endpoints. Step 3 closes the
`copy` graph over the NEW tag, step 4 scans the NEW tag alone, and steps 6 to
10 read the two nodes. Nothing in any step consults a release in between. So a
change made and then undone between the endpoints is not reported -- and that
is not a hole: such a locale ends with its data identical at both ends, so it
stands exactly where a locale that never changed stands, cleared by the data
diff and still subject to steps 4 and 5 and to the node steps 6 to 8 like
every other. NOT "it sorts the same on both systems": identical data is not
identical order, which is what the !! blocks below say and what step 5's 75
hunks are about. And an undone upstream change only reaches a real system if a
distro backported the intermediate state, which is the question steps 6 to 8
answer by comparing the nodes' own files -- not steps 4 and 5's question.

Measured over this span rather than argued: of the 350 locale files that
differ in at least one of the two steps, 349 also differ between glibc-2.28
and glibc-2.39. The one that does not is aa_ER@saaho, renamed to ssy_ER at
2.39, and the direct run reports it twice over -- as a rename in step 2 and as
a removed locale in step 8.

WHAT THIS IS NOT

One triple is not every triple. This is glibc 2.28, 2.34 and 2.39; it is
evidence about those three and about the method's shape, not a claim about
every pair of releases somebody might skip.

And it is the answer for a DIRECT upgrade. If you are going to run production
on the intermediate release for any length of time, audit each step: a locale
that changes on the way in and changes back on the way out needs a reindex at
each end, and a single run against the two outer versions is silent about it
because there is nothing there to see.

Long comma-separated name lists below are trimmed by the tool, marked where
cut. Step 5's hunk bodies are not reproduced: it prints 75 hunks over this
pair and decides none of them, which is the fifth limitation rather than this
one. Its per-file table is kept, since that is where the 75 comes from.
